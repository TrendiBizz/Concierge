THE SMALL BUSINESS FIELD GUIDE

Do B2B software companies block AI crawlers? robots.txt and llms.txt on 100 well-known sites (2026)

On 2 Nov 2026 we read the robots.txt and llms.txt files of 100 well-known B2B software sites in the US, UK, Australia and New Zealand. Of the 94 sites whose robots.txt we could read, 2 blocked any of 12 major AI crawlers at the site root, and neither blocked a search or user-fetch crawler. A re-fetch on 4 Nov 2026 confirmed this. 35 of 100 publish an llms.txt. The sample is small and list-based, and robots.txt is a request, not enforcement.

By Trendi · Published 2 November 2026 · Data fetched 2 November 2026, re-fetched 4 November 2026 (New Zealand time) · living page: refreshed monthly, next refresh December 2026

Key findings

All figures are from a snapshot on 2 Nov 2026, re-fetched on 4 Nov 2026 (New Zealand time, UTC+13); figures shown are from the 4 Nov re-fetch. Intervals are Wilson 95%. "Readable" means robots.txt answered: a real file, or a 404, empty or HTML response, which crawlers treat as "no rules". Readable n = 94 (US 23, UK 25, AU 23, NZ 23). The other 6 sites refused or challenged our request and are reported separately, never as allowed.

  1. Only 2 of 94 readable sites (2.1%, CI 0.6–7.4%) block any of the 12 major AI crawlers at the site root, on 2 Nov 2026.

    • notion.com blocks Meta-ExternalAgent and Amazonbot.
    • swipedon.com blocks CCBot and Bytespider.
    • By market: US 1/23, UK 0/25, AU 0/23, NZ 1/23.

  2. 0 of 94 readable sites (0%, CI 0–3.9%) block GPTBot, ClaudeBot, Google-Extended, OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, ChatGPT-User or Perplexity-User at the root, on 2 Nov 2026.

    • The two blocks in finding 1 target training or data crawlers only: Meta-ExternalAgent, Amazonbot, CCBot and Bytespider.
    • 0 of 94 block any search or user-fetch crawler (OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, ChatGPT-User, Perplexity-User, Claude-SearchBot, Claude-User).

  3. Most sites never name an AI crawler: 83 of 94 (88.3%) give AI crawlers the same rules as every other bot, on 2 Nov 2026.

    • GPTBot's verdict came from the * group on 68 sites and from no group on 9. The other 6 have no robots.txt.
    • 11 of 94 (11.7%, CI 6.7–19.8%) name at least one of the 12 crawlers. Of those 11:
      • 8 name GPTBot with an explicit Allow: / (deel, getsona, lawvu, modoenergy, navan, nextdc, swipedon, tanium).
      • 2 list AI crawlers in a Squarespace group that only carries the standard path rules (adarga.ai, netlinkz.com).
      • 1 names crawlers in order to block them (notion.com).

  4. 35 of 100 sites (35%, CI 26.4–44.7%) publish a real llms.txt, on 4 Nov 2026.

    • By market: US 13/25, UK 11/25, NZ 7/25, AU 4/25.
    • "Real" means HTTP 200, not HTML, at least 50 characters, and containing a markdown heading or URL. We read all of them by hand.
    • Having an llms.txt is a signal of intent, not proof of any effect.

  5. 0 of 94 readable sites (CI 0–3.9%) block Googlebot at the root, on 2 Nov 2026.

    • 2 of 94 give Googlebot its own named group (perplexity.ai, rpmglobal.com).
    • Among these sites, blocking AI crawlers while admitting Google is not a pattern. Mostly they treat every bot the same.

  6. 6 of 100 sites (6%) refused or challenged our plainly labelled request for robots.txt, on 2 Nov 2026.

    • 403: gusto.com, cohesity.com, technologyonecorp.com.
    • 429: gotracksuit.com.
    • 202 bot challenge: alcidion.com, optimalworkshop.com.
    • By market: US 2, UK 0, AU 2, NZ 2.
    • What a WAF does to named AI crawlers can differ from what robots.txt says, and our method cannot see that. Treat finding 1 as a floor for stated blocking, not total blocking.

  7. 4 of 94 readable robots.txt files (4.3%, CI 1.7–10.4%) carry a Cloudflare-style Content-Signal line, on 2 Nov 2026 (deel.com, rippling.com, tanium.com, ascendx.cloud).

    • Content-Signal states usage preferences such as search, ai-input and ai-train, separately from access.
    • It is a newer convention and not part of the robots.txt standard (RFC 9309).

What this means

  • Among these 100 well-known B2B software firms on 2 and 4 Nov 2026, stated robots.txt blocking of major AI crawlers was rare (2 of 94 readable), and about a third (35 of 100) publish an llms.txt.
  • This does not show that the pattern holds for all B2B firms, and it says nothing about WAF or CDN blocking.
  • It does not show that llms.txt, or open robots.txt rules, lead to AI mentions, traffic or rankings. We make no such claim and these data cannot support one.
  • If you manage a site, the practical step is to check what your own robots.txt says about AI crawlers and whether that matches your intent. What you choose is a business decision for your organisation.
  • To check your own site, run the AI crawler checker. It reads what your robots.txt says about AI crawlers.

Context

Two vendors have published research on how B2B software buyers use AI tools. Both G2 and AirOps have a commercial interest in the topic. Their figures are quoted as published, and we did not verify them.

  • In G2's March 2026 survey of 1,076 B2B software buyers, 51% said they start software research with an AI chatbot more often than with Google. Asked which sources influence their shortlist, 54% of buyers in G2's 2026 survey named AI chatbots, ahead of review sites (43%), vendor websites (36%) and vendor salespeople (19%). Source: G2, The Answer Economy (2026 AI Search Insight Report), 15 April 2026, https://learn.g2.com/g2-2026-ai-search-insight-report
  • In AirOps' October 2025 analysis of 21,311 brand mentions in ChatGPT, Claude and Perplexity answers to software discovery queries, 85% were attributed to third-party sites and 13.2% to the brand's own site. Source: AirOps, The Influence of Offsite Signals in AI Search, 17 Oct 2025, https://www.airops.com/report/the-influence-of-offsite-signals-in-ai-search

Method in brief

  • Who: 100 companies, 25 per market (US, UK, AU, NZ), taken from public lists rather than chosen by hand: Forbes Cloud 100 (2025) for the US, Tech Nation Future Fifty (2026) for the UK, the S&P/ASX All Technology Index for Australia, and KiwiSaaS and LeadWave NZ SaaS lists for New Zealand. Exclusion rules: for the US, the first 25 US-headquartered companies by rank (Canva, Celonis and Miro skipped); for the UK, B2B software members only (Automata dropped as hardware); for Australia, software, IT and health-IT only (Xero excluded as NZ-based, Life360 as consumer, and Fineos, Yojee and Mach7 as headquartered elsewhere); for New Zealand, NZ-headquartered B2B only (Lightspeed and Xplor excluded as headquartered elsewhere, Serato as consumer). The data download lists each company's source list, rank and source URL.
  • What we fetched: /robots.txt and /llms.txt for each domain, plus the home page title. We used an honest user agent (TrendiResearch/1.0, with a link to trendibizz.com), 5 requests at a time, a 10-second timeout and up to 5 redirects. Two passes ran on 2 Nov 2026 (New Zealand time, UTC+13).
  • What counts as blocking: A crawler counts as blocked when the robots.txt rules that apply to it disallow the site root /. Partial means some path is disallowed but / is allowed. Sites that refused our request are not counted either way.
  • How robots.txt was read: to RFC 9309. Matching groups are merged, the longest matching path wins, and Allow wins a tie. Verdicts are judged at the site root / for 12 AI crawlers, with Googlebot as a control. A check against Protego (Scrapy's parser) agreed on 1,320 of 1,320 crawler-by-site checks.
  • Crawler selection rule: The 12 headline AI crawlers are the list fixed in the study plan, taken from Trendi's robots checker (/tools/robots-ai-check): training/data, search and user-triggered agents published by OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Apple, Common Crawl, ByteDance, Meta, Amazon and Perplexity. Googlebot is the control. The checker also lists Claude-SearchBot and Claude-User; the plan left them out, so they are scored as extra columns, not part of the headline set. The list is not exhaustive.
  • QA: a same-day re-fetch gave identical results for every domain that served a real robots.txt in both passes, and a 48-hour re-fetch on 4 Nov 2026 changed only 2 rows (cohesity.com began refusing our request; staah.com began answering). We read by hand every robots.txt that names an AI crawler (11 sites), every llms.txt file, and the soft, empty and challenge responses.

Caveats

  • n is small (94 readable sites; 23–25 per market) and the intervals are wide.
  • Selection is list-based, not random. The sites are well known and mostly larger firms, so they do not represent all businesses.
  • The data comes from two fetches of the same 100 sites, on 2 and 4 Nov 2026, and sites change their robots.txt often.
  • robots.txt is a request, not enforcement. WAF or CDN blocking is invisible to this method, apart from the 6 sites that refused our own labelled request.
  • Verdicts are judged at the site root only. A "partial" result usually means the site keeps admin, search or preview paths private.
  • A 48-hour re-fetch of all 100 sites on 4 November 2026 (two passes, same script) changed 2 verdicts. The next full re-fetch is scheduled for December 2026.

FAQ

Does this show that AI crawlers can reach these sites? No. It shows what each site's robots.txt states at the root. Many sites also use a WAF or CDN that can block bots regardless of robots.txt, and this method cannot see that. 6 of the 100 sites refused or challenged even our labelled request.

Why are there 94 sites in the robots.txt figures, not 100? 6 sites answered our request with a 403, a 429 or a bot challenge, so we could not read their robots.txt. We report them separately and never count them as allowing or blocking.

Does publishing an llms.txt affect AI mentions, traffic or rankings? This study does not test that, and we make no claim about it. 35 of 100 sites publish an llms.txt, and having one is a signal of intent, not evidence of any effect.
